Field-scale sensor drift in soil-moisture telemetry: a two-season study

Abstract. Soil-moisture probes are the cheapest way to close the irrigation
loop, and the least trustworthy. We instrumented 48 probes across four
orchards for two full seasons, cross-checked every reading against gravimetric
samples taken weekly, and characterise how the relationship between probe
output and true volumetric water content changes over time. Drift is not random
noise around a stable calibration: it is a slow, monotone bias that reaches 4.1
percentage points of volumetric water content after 14 months in the worst
sensor, and it is strongly correlated with cumulative soil temperature and with
the number of wet-dry cycles the probe has seen.

1. Introduction. An irrigation decision is a threshold decision. If the probe
reads 22% when the field is at 18%, the controller delays watering by a day and
the crop pays for it. Manufacturers specify accuracy at commissioning, not at
month fourteen, and farm telemetry pipelines almost never re-calibrate because
re-calibration means digging the probe out. The result is that a fleet of
sensors reports a coherent-looking time series in which the drift is invisible:
each probe is internally consistent, and only the cross-check against physical
samples reveals the bias.

=== PAGE 2 ===
2. Instrumentation. Each site carries 12 probes at three depths (10, 30 and 60
centimetres), sampled every 15 minutes and transmitted over LoRaWAN. Weekly
gravimetric samples were taken at three points per site with a 0-10 cm auger,
oven-dried at 105 degrees Celsius for 24 hours, and converted to volumetric
water content using measured bulk density. 2,304 gravimetric samples were
collected over the two seasons. Soil temperature at 10 centimetres was logged
by the same probe housing and is used here as the drift covariate.

3. Calibration procedure. At commissioning each probe was calibrated against
the first four gravimetric samples using a two-point linear fit, which is the
standard field practice. We then refit the same two-point model every eight
weeks and record the change in slope and offset. A probe is declared drifting
when the refitted slope differs from the commissioning slope by more than 10%.

=== PAGE 3 ===
4. Results. 31 of the 48 probes drifted beyond the 10% slope criterion at least
once; 12 drifted in both seasons. Median time to first drift detection was 9.5
months. The worst sensor accumulated 4.1 percentage points of bias at 30
centimetres after 14 months, enough to move an irrigation trigger from 22% to
26% and delay watering by roughly one day. Drift magnitude correlates with
cumulative soil temperature above 15 degrees (Spearman rho 0.62) and with the
count of wet-dry cycles (rho 0.48), and correlates only weakly with probe age
alone (rho 0.21), which is why replacement schedules based on age perform poorly.
Shallow probes drifted more than deep probes: 10-centimetre probes accounted for
19 of the 31 detections.

5. Discussion. Because drift is monotone, a single mid-season gravimetric check
recovers most of the accuracy: refitting after one sample reduced mean absolute
error from 3.2 to 1.4 percentage points in our fleet. The operational advice is
therefore not better hardware but one physical sample per probe per season, plus
a slope term in the telemetry database so that the correction is applied at read
time rather than baked into stored values.

=== PAGE 4 ===
6. Limitations. Four orchards in one climatic zone, two seasons, 48 probes: the
correlations are suggestive rather than predictive, and the drift coefficients
should not be extrapolated to other soil textures without re-measurement. The
gravimetric reference itself carries about 0.8 percentage points of error from
bulk-density variation, which sets a floor under every number reported here.
Radio outages removed 3.7% of the expected readings; we interpolated gaps
shorter than six hours and dropped longer ones, which biases the wet-season
coverage slightly downward. We did not test capacitive probes from more than one
manufacturer, and we did not measure whether drift continues past 14 months,
because no probe in the fleet ran that long without being serviced.
