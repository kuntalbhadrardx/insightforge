Contamination thresholds in crop-pest image classifiers: an empirical audit

Abstract. Pest-detection models are usually trained on field imagery that was
labelled by contractors under time pressure, so a share of the training labels
is wrong in a way nobody recorded. We audit three public crop-pest datasets and
one in-house potato late-blight dataset, report how much of each label set the
authors of the original release had already flagged as suspect, and measure what
happens to downstream precision when that contamination is modelled explicitly
rather than assumed away. The audit covers 41,388 images across four datasets.
We find contamination between 2.1% and 12.4% depending on dataset and crop, and
show that a model trained without an explicit contamination term overstates its
own precision by 3 to 6 points on a clean holdout.

1. Introduction. A pest classifier deployed on a farm is asked a narrow
question — is this leaf damaged by the target pest or not — and it is asked it
thousands of times a day, mostly about easy cases. The cost of an error is
asymmetric: a missed infestation costs a spray cycle, a false alarm costs a
wasted tank. Both errors are cheap individually and expensive in aggregate,
which is why the reported precision of these models matters more than their
top-line accuracy. The literature reports precision on curated test sets that
were labelled by the same contractors who labelled training data, so the number
is measured on the distribution the model already fits. This paper is about
what changes when that assumption is removed.

2. Data. The four datasets are PlantVillage (54,305 images, 38 classes, studio
backgrounds), PestImg-21 (9,104 field images, 21 classes), RicePest-BD (3,220
images captured in Bangladesh during two seasons) and an in-house potato
late-blight set (4,764 images captured on three farms between 2019 and 2022).
We use 41,388 of these images after discarding exact duplicates and images whose
EXIF timestamp is missing. Each dataset shipped with a per-release audit file
listing images the original authors had already marked as uncertain, mislabelled
or duplicated. Those audit files are the closest thing to ground truth about
contamination that exists, and they are almost never used in published
benchmarks.

3. Method. We fit a logistic model of label correctness against capture
metadata — hour of day, device model, farm, season, and the annotator's median
time per image — and use its predicted probability as a per-image contamination
weight. Training then minimises a weighted cross-entropy in which each image's
loss is multiplied by one minus its estimated contamination probability. We
compare this against the same architecture trained on the same split with no
weighting. Precision is reported on a 2,000-image holdout that was relabelled
from scratch by two independent annotators, with disagreements adjudicated by a
third; this holdout is the only part of the study where labels were produced
without knowledge of the model.

=== PAGE 3 ===
4. Results. Contamination is not spread evenly. PlantVillage, captured indoors
against uniform backgrounds, carries the lowest rate at 2.1%. PestImg-21 sits at
6.8%, RicePest-BD at 9.9%, and the in-house potato late-blight set carries the
highest rate at 12.4%. In the late-blight set the contamination clusters sharply
by capture condition: 71% of the flagged images were captured between 19:00 and
05:00 under LED lighting, where the lesion colour that annotators key on shifts
toward brown and becomes indistinguishable from soil splash. Daytime images from
the same farms carry a 4.3% rate. Weighting recovers 3.1 points of precision on
the clean holdout for the late-blight model, 4.8 for RicePest-BD and 6.0 for
PestImg-21; PlantVillage, being nearly clean, moves by less than half a point.

5. Discussion. The practical lesson is that contamination is a property of the
capture protocol, not of the crop. A dataset assembled from daylight hand-held
photos and a dataset assembled from overnight trap cameras differ by a factor of
three in label error even when the same annotators and the same guidelines are
used. Reporting precision without also reporting the estimated contamination
rate therefore hides the largest single source of variance between studies. We
recommend that releases publish their audit file alongside the images so that
downstream users can weight rather than guess.

=== PAGE 4 ===
6. Limitations. The contamination weights come from metadata, so any error mode
that is uncorrelated with capture time, device, farm or annotation speed is
invisible to this method. The in-house set is small and comes from three farms
in one region, so its 12.4% figure should not be read as a national estimate.
The clean holdout, while independently labelled, contains only 2,000 images and
its precision estimates carry roughly ±1.3 points at 95% confidence. We did not
evaluate detection models (bounding boxes), only whole-image classification, and
we did not test whether the weighting scheme transfers to a transformer
backbone. Finally, the audit files we rely on are self-reported by dataset
authors; a release that never audited its labels would appear clean to this
method even if it were not.
